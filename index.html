WITHIN TOLERANCE ABOVE TOLERANCE
01 CONTEXT

System quality and project performance were measured through Service-Level Agreements — time-based KPIs, principally Time to Resolution. It was the metric everyone reported, and the one nobody trusted.

02 PROBLEM

A time-based SLA measures how fast we respond. It says nothing about how good the system is — and it can be gamed by the calendar.

a SLAs measure response, not quality — a team could hit every target while the system stayed fragile.
b A fast fix means nothing if the defects keep coming back — speed was beside the point.
03 APPROACH

The goal: a metric that reflects true system quality, works fairly across project sizes, and is simple enough to enforce.

The opening hypothesis was the obvious one: bigger projects should naturally carry more defects, so project value could be used to normalise the count. Testing it honestly is what made the rest of the work necessary. Use the three views above to follow the argument.

CORRELATION TEST Z-SCORE ATTEMPT DPMR MAD KPI BANDS
04 SOLUTION

Two pieces, in sequence. First a normalised measure — Defects Per Million Ringgit — so projects of any size become directly comparable on defect density. Then a robust benchmark, because the normalised data was still too skewed for a mean or a z-score to describe.

THE NORMALISATION
DPMR = ( Production Defects / PO Value ) × 1,000,000

Defect density per million of contract value. A small project with twenty defects and a strategic programme with eighty can finally be judged on the same axis.

07 REFLECTION

The most useful thing I did was disprove my own hypothesis. Project value felt like the obvious normaliser — the data said no, and binning and percentile scoring went with it.

MAD earned its place in the analysis but not the final score. A KPI that needs a statistics lesson to defend won't survive its first governance meeting — so the version that shipped is the plainest one still defensible.

01 CONTEXT

Portfolio profit and loss, revenue forecast, resource lists and rate cards lived in four separate workbooks, each owned by a different person and each shaped slightly differently every month.

02 PROBLEM

The analysis was scattered and entirely manual. It took hours, it broke quietly, and by the time management saw a number the month it described was already gone.

a Hours of copy-paste per cycle, with every merge and mapping done by hand — so errors were not unlikely, they were expected.
b Management could only ask questions the last report had already answered. Anything else meant a request, a queue and another cycle of lag.
c Nobody could test a decision before making it. “What if we add two seniors in November?” had no answer short of rebuilding the model.
03 APPROACH

Automate the consolidation first, then design the interface around the four questions management actually asked — how did we do, what is coming, who do we have, and what should we change.

MULTI-HEADER INGEST MODULE ↔ PROJECT MAPPING GROUPED ACCOUNTS RATE CARD × MANDAYS SELF-SERVE SIMULATION
04 SOLUTION

One application, four modules, a single load of the source files. Performance and Optimisation are the two above; Forecast and Headcount sit behind the same navigation.

THE COST MODEL
Burn rate = Σ ( rate[grade, origin] × 20 mandays )
Allowable cost = Total revenue × ( 1 − target GPM )
Excess headcount = Excess cost / ( avg MD rate × 20 × months left )

Three lines that turn a headcount argument into an arithmetic one. Excess cost stops being a feeling about a team being “heavy” and becomes a number with a rate card behind it.

05 IMPACT

Analysis that took hours now takes minutes, and management no longer waits for it. They open the tool, filter to the account they care about, and run their own scenario.

06 REFLECTION

Most of the build was defensive: source columns arrive named differently every month, so the loaders resolve headers by pattern and fall back to safe defaults rather than failing.

The simulator earned its keep for a reason I did not plan for. Letting managers test their own “what if” ended most of the arguments about the numbers, because they could see exactly which assumption they disagreed with.

PORTRAIT OF JONATHAN

I'm an analytical problem solver and PMO Executive, working at the intersection of data, structure and strategy — breaking complex operational problems down and turning them into clear, actionable insight. Technology is a catalyst; the core of the work is analytical thinking, systems design and problem decomposition.

At Hitachi eBworx I specialise in governance, audit and data analysis, improving project quality and efficiency through process improvement and measurement people can actually act on. Most of that work is proprietary, so what I publish are sanitised case studies that show the thinking rather than the client.

Outside that, I photograph people and events, mostly in available light, and I write essays about certainty, measurement and the odd places where the two disagree. The three habits reinforce each other more than they compete.
